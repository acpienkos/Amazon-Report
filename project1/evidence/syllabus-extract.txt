Teaching Philosophy
AI makes answers cheap; it does not make understanding, judgment, or growth
automatic. The scarce work shifts to choosing questions worth answering, deciding
what to trust and do, and developing the capacity to own the consequences. My role is
not to hand you finished answers or complete code. I design problems, tools, and
feedback loops that let you build, test, fail, explain, and improve. That is why this course
emphasizes the D stage of DRIVER: discover and define the right problem before AI
accelerates the wrong one. We build to learn—not learn merely to build. The goal is not
to outperform AI. It is to use AI as a studio for developing your own understanding,
judgment, and capacity to act.
Growth and wisdom matter deeply, but I do not grade them as personality traits or ask
you to perform transformation. I grade visible acts of judgment: asking better questions,
surfacing assumptions, calibrating confidence to evidence, testing independently,
revising after failure, naming limitations, and transferring what you learned to a new
problem. You are not penalized for where you begin or rewarded for merely claiming
growth.
How to Succeed in this Course
Start with D: discover and define. Before you build, write down the problem, who it is for,
what you know, what you need to learn, the real constraints, and what success looks
like. A polished solution to the wrong problem is still failure.
Use the best available AI models and harnesses—but do not confuse capability with
reliability. Working at the frontier reveals both AI’s capacity and its shortcomings. Treat
every output as an experiment: prompt, run, inspect, challenge, revise, and document.
Test it against known answers, reasonable ranges, edge cases, and a second
independent model. Practice through many small cycles; failure is data. Never submit
work you cannot explain. Success is turning an AI-produced result into your own
defensible financial judgment.
Build locally from the beginning. Use AI to help you plan, write, debug, and test code,
but you must be able to run that code on your own computer, inspect errors, install
dependencies, manage a multi-file project, and turn analysis into a working web

What the criteria mean. Data integrity includes the correct company or security,
reporting period and as-of date, source, units, currency, definitions, transformations,
missing-data treatment, and reconciliation to authoritative data. Financial soundness
includes correct finance principles, internally consistent assumptions, accounting and
cash-flow treatment, time value of money, defensible discount rates, and economically
plausible constraints. For example, a perpetual growth rate must not exceed a
defensible long-run nominal growth rate for the relevant economy; no company can
outgrow the economy forever. Validation includes independent checks, known-answer
comparisons, sensitivity and stress testing, edge cases, and reproducibility. Technical
execution covers whether the application runs reliably and is usable. A polished
application cannot compensate for materially inaccurate data or financially impossible
assumptions. These criteria grade evidence in the work, not personality, confidence, or
claimed transformation.

Academic Integrity
Purdue prohibits dishonesty in connection with any University activity. Cheating,
plagiarism, and knowingly furnishing false information to the University are examples of
dishonesty (Part 5, Section III-B-2-a, University Regulations).
Specific to this course:
You are responsible for every submitted claim, assumption, output, and material code
path, regardless of whether AI produced it. Instructors may request a live demonstration
or oral explanation to assess authorship and understanding. Inability to explain the work
affects the relevant grading criteria and may prompt an academic-integrity review, but it
is not by itself an automatic misconduct finding; any misconduct determination follows
Purdue procedures.
Undisclosed AI use is an academic integrity violation. Disclosed AI use that contains
unedited AI text or factual errors is a quality problem and is graded down, not referred
— the difference is disclosure.
Collaboration is encouraged during in-class labs. Individual project submissions must be
your own work; sharing project deliverables between students is not permitted.
Knowingly submitting a lab checkout for a session you did not attend, submitting on
another student’s behalf, or falsifying teammates/learning partners, contribution, the in-
person attendance declaration, or evidence is also prohibited. A discrepancy is reviewed
rather than treated as automatic proof of misconduct.

AI Policy
AI use is required in this course, not merely permitted. Learning to direct AI toward
correct, verifiable financial analysis is the central skill being taught and assessed.
The same requirements apply to every student on every assignment:
Disclose. Every submission states which AI access path you used and for what. Record
only model identifiers the interface actually exposed. If you used a published packet or a
course-run response, name that path and do not claim its collection tools as your own
use. A vague or unnamed access path does not satisfy the disclosure element.
Document. Every project includes an AI-use and validation record — key prompts, what
the AI produced, and how you verified it — plus a reflection on what AI did well, where it
struggled, and what you checked independently.
Validate. Every AI-produced result is a hypothesis until you test it. Check it against a
known answer, check it for reasonableness, check the edge cases, and check for the
failure modes specific to AI, such as fabricated sources and confidently wrong figures.
This course requires comparison of two independent system outputs—through packets,
available free interfaces, a funded Purdue route if announced, or course-run responses
—partly so you can cross-examine one with the other.
Own it. You are responsible for everything you submit. AI is a collaborator, not an
author, and human judgment stays central to every financial decision you present.
